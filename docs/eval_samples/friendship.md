Alice and Bob and the Crooked Kite

Alice woke up with a messy braid and a big smile. Bob was already waiting at the foot of her bed. He was a gray cat with a white patch on one paw, shaped just like a mitten.

"Good morning, Bob," said Alice. "Today is the village fair!"

Bob stretched, yawned, and went, "Mrrp." That meant, *Let's go!*

In the garden, the sun felt warm on their backs. Alice held up her kite. It was made of brown paper, with two thin sticks crossed in the middle. One corner was a little bent, like a sleepy smile.

"Isn't it wonderful?" Alice asked.

Bob sniffed the paper. Then he sat right down on top of it.

Alice giggled. "Okay, you can be its guard."

At the fair, the kite contest was loud and bright. Children held up kites painted with stars and rainbows. Their kites were shiny and straight as rulers. Ribbon tails fluttered everywhere.

"Whoa, look at that one!" said a boy.

"Mine has golden tails!" said a girl.

Alice's tummy felt wobbly. Slowly, she slid her crooked kite behind her back. "It will look silly next to theirs," she whispered.

She walked to the old wooden fence and sat down all by herself. The kite stayed hidden behind her.

Bob hopped up beside her. He did not ask any questions. He just sat close, so close that his soft fur brushed her arm. He purred, low and steady, like a little engine. Then he looked at the fair, and then he looked at Alice, and he waited.

Alice sighed. "Bob, I'm scared," she said at last. "My kite is crooked. Everyone else's is perfect. Mine will look silly when it flies."

Bob blinked his slow, sleepy blink. Then he gently tapped her hand with his mitten paw.

Alice looked at her kite. It did not look so silly now. It looked like *her* kite.

"Would you come fly it with me?" she asked.

Bob jumped down and trotted toward the open field. His tail stood straight up like a flag. That was a yes!

Alice took a deep breath. She walked out into the grass, held the kite high, and felt the wind tug at it. Her heart went *thump, thump*. Then she let go.

The kite wobbled. It tipped left. It tipped right.

"Oh no," Alice gasped.

Bob sat in the grass beside her. He did not move. He just watched, very still and very brave.

Then a gust of wind lifted the kite higher. It climbed, and climbed, and began to dance, swooping and bobbing against the blue sky.

"Look!" shouted a little girl. "It's zigzagging!"

Other children came running. "Can I try?" "It's so cool!"

Alice laughed. It came out bubbly and bright. She looked down at Bob, who was grinning in his cat way, and she knew she could not have done it alone.

Later, as the sun turned golden, Alice and Bob walked home together. Alice swung the kite over her shoulder.

"What was your favorite part today?" she asked.

Bob chirped softly.

"Mine was when the kite danced," said Alice. "And when you sat with me by the fence. Next time, let's make a kite with a silver tail."

Bob purred. That meant, *Yes, please.*

"Crooked kites still fly, friend," Alice said, and she smiled.

Back in their cozy room, Alice set the kite on its shelf. Bob curled up on the windowsill. Alice climbed up beside him.

Outside, the stars came out one by one. The evening breeze hummed softly.

Alice leaned against Bob's warm side. Bob tucked his mitten paw under his chin.

The crooked kite rested safely on the shelf.

Alice and Bob snuggled together by the window.

Their breathing slowed.

Soft. Slow. Sleepy.

The breeze hummed on.

Goodnight, Alice. Goodnight, Bob.

**Alice and Bob**

Alice had lived on Maple Street for as long as she could remember, and for almost all of that time, she'd had a best friend named Bob.

Bob was a cat. A large orange tabby with one white paw and a crooked tail that curled like a question mark. He had appeared on her porch the summer she turned seven, yowling at the screen door as if he'd been waiting for her specifically, and from that day on he was hers, or she was his. They had never quite agreed on which.

Every morning, Bob walked her to the bus stop. He never came past the corner of Maple and Elm, because that was where his territory ended, and he took the boundary very seriously. Alice would wave to him from the bus window, and he would sit perfectly upright on the curb, tail wrapped neatly around his paws, watching until the bus turned out of sight.

Every afternoon, he was waiting for her on the wall by the mailboxes, and they would walk home together, Alice telling him everything that had happened at school. Bob never judged her spelling tests or her terrible attempts at the recorder. He simply listened, blinking slowly, and occasionally headbutted her knee when she said something he approved of.

Some people on the street thought it was strange, a girl talking to a cat as though he understood every word. Alice didn't mind. She had a theory, which she had explained to her mother once, that Bob understood more than people gave him credit for. "He just doesn't see the point in saying it out loud," she'd said. Her mother had laughed and agreed that this was probably true of most cats.

But the summer Alice turned twelve, things began to change.

Bob started going missing for hours at a time. He would be gone after breakfast and not return until the streetlights came on, sometimes with burrs in his fur or a smudge of mud on his white paw. Alice worried. She searched the yards along Elm Street and called his name from the edge of the park. Each time, he would eventually stroll up the sidewalk as though nothing unusual had happened, and she would scold him, and he would purr loudly and refuse to look at her.

One evening in August, she followed him.

It was harder than she expected. Bob moved like a shadow, slipping under hedges and along fence tops, and twice she lost him entirely. But she kept going, past the park, past the old railway bridge, down a gravel path she had never noticed before, until she came to a wall of overgrown ivy at the edge of an abandoned lot.

Bob was sitting in front of it. When he saw her, he didn't look guilty. He looked, if anything, a little pleased with himself.

He pushed his head through a gap in the ivy and meowed once, sharply.

Alice pushed the leaves aside.

Behind them was a garden. It was small and wild and full of color, with tall sunflowers, rows of strange glowing mushrooms, and an old stone fountain in the center where water moved in slow spirals instead of falling. A narrow path wound between the flowers, and at the far end sat a weathered wooden bench, where a tiny silver-haired woman in a patched cardigan was pouring tea into two cups.

"Ah," she said, without looking up. "You found it. Good. Bob has been very eager for you to see it."

Alice turned to look at her cat. Bob sat down on the path and began to wash his white paw, as if he had no idea what she was talking about.

"You can talk?" Alice whispered.

"Oh, *he* can't," said the old woman, smiling. "That's the funny thing about cats. They always know more than they say. But you, my dear, have always been very good at listening."

Alice stepped through the ivy and into the garden, and behind her, quietly, the leaves closed back over the gap, leaving only the quiet hum of the fountain and the sound of a cat purring somewhere close by.
