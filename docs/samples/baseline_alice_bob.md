**Alice and Bob**

Alice had lived on Maple Street for nine years, long enough to know every crack in the sidewalk and every dog that barked behind every fence. But she had only had Bob for three months, and in that short time he had become the most important thing in her world.

Bob was a gray tabby with one white paw and a torn left ear, the kind of cat who looked like he had seen things. He had shown up on her back porch one rainy October evening, soaked and yowling, and had refused to leave. Her mother said he belonged to someone else. Her mother was wrong, Alice decided, because nobody who loved a cat would let him wander around in the rain looking like that.

No one came to claim him, so Bob became hers.

Every morning, Alice walked to the bus stop with Bob trotting behind her. He never followed her past the corner of Maple and Elm, but he always waited there, sitting neatly on the curb, tail wrapped around his front paws, until the yellow bus turned the bend. Then he would stroll back home as if he had important cat business to attend to.

Every afternoon, he was waiting for her again, on the same curb, as though he had never left.

"You're going to get yourself run over," Alice told him one day, scratching behind his torn ear.

Bob blinked slowly, which Alice had decided meant *I'm not worried about it, and neither should you.*

They shared everything. Alice read her library books aloud on the porch, and Bob listened with his eyes half-closed. When she was sad about a math test, he climbed into her lap and purred until the knot in her stomach loosened. When she built a cardboard fort in the backyard, he claimed the top floor and refused to come down for dinner. When she cried the night her grandfather died, Bob didn't say anything, because he couldn't, but he stayed curled against her side until morning.

That was the thing about Bob. He never needed to talk to be a good friend.

In the spring, something changed. Bob started disappearing for longer stretches, slipping out the back door before dawn and returning only at dusk, his fur burred with twigs and his one white paw muddy. Alice worried. She imagined him lost, or stuck, or hurt.

One Saturday, she decided to follow him.

Bob led her across three backyards, under a broken fence, and through a tangle of blackberry bushes at the edge of the creek. Alice scratched her arms and nearly lost a sneaker in the mud, but she kept going until the trees opened into a small clearing.

There, beneath an old oak, sat a scruffy orange cat with a bent tail and three hungry-looking kittens tumbling over one another in a nest of dry leaves.

Bob walked right up to the orange cat and rubbed his head against her shoulder. The orange cat purred.

Alice stared. "Bob," she whispered, "you have a family."

Bob glanced back at her, then turned and walked to Alice's feet. He sat down, looked up at her, and gave a small, decisive meow.

Alice understood. He wasn't leaving her. He had simply been making sure the mother cat and her kittens had food, a dry place to sleep, and someone to look out for them. Every day, he had been doing what she did for him: keeping watch.

"Okay," Alice said softly, kneeling down. "We'll help them together."

That evening, Alice carried a bowl of food and an old blanket down to the creek, Bob trotting proudly at her side. They set up a little shelter beneath the oak, and the orange cat watched them with wary but grateful eyes.

When they walked home in the golden light, Bob stayed right beside her the entire way, not one step behind, not one step ahead.

Alice smiled and reached down to rub his torn ear.

"Best friends," she said.

Bob purred, and this time, Alice was sure he meant it too.
